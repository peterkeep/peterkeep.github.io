It's hard to remember, but there are more of us than I sometimes think. Every time, I get surprised when there are more of us than just me and the people I confide in.

Ok, let's back up and add some context. A handful of faculty at my college were awarded a grant to work on some open educational resources for our courses. I talked about this a bit before, but it's the grant that allowed me to write [*Discover Calculus*](https://www.discovercalculus.com). Anyways, the grant has concluded and projects are "finished" (as if anything in the OER space is ever fully finished), and now we're being asked to talk about our experiences or projects every now and then. So I guess I wasn't too surprised when we were each asked to give a short, 3 minute update on our projects at the yearly All-Faculty meeting this Fall semester.

The All-Faculty meeting is a yearly chance for the Vice President of Academic Affairs to set an agenda to address whatever major topics they'd like to designate as focal points for the academic year. This year, the topics were Accessibility, Assessment, Affordability, and AI. The faculty from the OER grant would represent the "Affordability" part. All of this to say: I had to think about what to say for 3 minutes, a length of time that is entirely too short to say anything meaningful but too long to stand silently in front of the faculty and smile and wave. So I had to say something. 

I decided against giving an overview of the content in my book. Most of our faculty doesn't care about calculus, or my specific presentation of its Fundamental Theorem.

(Ok, I actually did show that part. I decided to put a QR code leading to the activity I use to demonstrate the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus so that people could play with it instead of listening if they wanted to.)

{: .caption}
![Fundamental Theorem of Calculus slide.]({{ '/assets/img/posts/FTC-act.png' | relative_url }})
*The slide with the link to the [Fundamental Theorem of Calculus activity](https://www.discovercalculus.com/web/sec-FTOC.html#act-FTOC1).*

So instead, I showed off the cover, talked about some goals that I had while creating the book, and then ended with a snippet of my [Disclosure on the use of Generative AI](https://www.discovercalculus.com/web/Preface-NoteOnAI.html). I didn't read it, but I said:

> I believe that there are no ethical use cases of generative AI in education. More broadly, I don't think there are ethical use cases of generative AI in our regular lives, either.

{: .caption}
![Disclosure on the Use of AI slide.]({{ '/assets/img/posts/disclosure.png' | relative_url }})
*The slide with the link to the [AI Disclosure](https://www.discovercalculus.com/web/Preface-NoteOnAI.html).*

And then I said, to close, that I was glad that my project reflected these values that I hold. And that was it! I was done! I sat down on my chair on the stage while another group of faculty talked about their project, and the meeting moved on. I thought.

After we all sat back down, the last section of the meeting was devoted to updates from the AI Teaching and Learning Committee. It started off a little awkward.

> AI in education is a divisive topic. It's not easy to talk about because people have such strong opinions and are not willing to listen to each other. There are some faculty that love AI and want to use it for everything, and there are other faculty that say that any use of AI is unethical. That makes our job hard.

With a pointed look towards me, it was hard to miss that my comments weren't well received. I tried to just sit calmly and shut up, and eventually slipped out a bit early. In the parking lot later, one of the English faculty told me that he was surprised that the writing guy and the calculus guy have so much in common. I went home and tried to forget the awkwardness of it all. 

Over the next few days, though, I kept running into reminders that wouldn't let me forget.

- A text from a campus friend about saying the stuff that they didn't get an opportunity.
- An email encouraging me about speaking out.
- A faculty member that interrupted their class as I walked by to tell me that it was important that we say what needs to be said.
- A dean telling me that they loved my part of the presentation and were happy to hear what I said.
- Another email thanking me for saying the things that they had assumed they were alone in thinking.

It turns out that we weren't alone! There are more of us than I think there are! I sent back an email saying thank you for the encouragement, and added:

> It's a shame to let the college run as an individual institution directed by a small group of administrators instead of as a collective of faculty...I tried to remind myself that it's good to speak up about the stuff I'm passionate about, even if it's unpopular with admin because we (the faculty) should be involved more in steering the direction of the college.

And that's really it. What I thought of as a small act of resistance, just because I needed to say something for my own sense of self, was really something that several of us, more than I remembered, felt. I was able to speak on behalf of others. And all it took was a small slide at the end of a 3 minute update on my book. Honestly, the tiniest of lifts on my part.

What are the other campus issues that seem to be driven by a singular force instead of faculty consensus? Is it the resources that trans students have (or don't have!) on campus? Is it the percentage of the college endowment invested in weapons manufacturing? Is it the use of college campus space by the county sheriff's department? Maybe it's standing up for the academic freedom of other faculty with classes that cover some of the typical right-wing culture war topics.

In each of these, we are often led to believe that the institution as an imbued with some unstoppable momentum. But in reality, there are more of us ready to resist than we sometimes think. We just need to start.
