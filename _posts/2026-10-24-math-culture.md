*August 30, 2026*[^class]

The semester is starting well! I’m teaching familiar classes and I’m getting to know unfamiliar students. Each group of students is different, and each semester has its own unique characteristics, but some things are consistent. Here’s one: After class this week, one of the students in my class hung around to ask about some class policies and grade stuff. "What’s the best way to do homework in this class?" they asked.

I’ll get more of these consistent questions soon: "What’s the best way to study?" or "What else should I be doing at home?" or even "Am I taking notes the right way?" These questions aren’t unique to my classes. Students everywhere are trying, in their introduction to college, to figure out what it means to be a college student. This isn’t going to be a note about students where I start complaining about "kids these days" and "college readiness" and "back when I was a student." Instead, it’s a note where I acknowledge the immense amount of responsibility that we, as instructors, are given. Responsibility to help guide students towards what I believe are the kinds of habits and practices that are helpful to students. I’m given a chance to build a culture using more than just my behavior in class: I get to do this directly with students. They ask "how should I be a student in this class?" and I get to tell them explicitly!

So what do we tell students? What do we model for students? What do we encourage them to do inside our classroom or when they’re at home thinking about our class?

I can talk about what I’ve been telling students...that making mistakes (and using them as a catalyst for internalizing big ideas) is an essential part of "doing mathematics." That practicing problems is most helpful when you explicitly connect each problem to a big result or idea from the course. That doing mathematics is best done in community with others. That mathematics is more about clear and precise communication than it is about long calculations, and so practicing communicating is at least as important as practicing calculating. 

It’s great that I get to tell students these things every semester as they work on figuring out how to be a student in a college math class. But telling them these things can’t be enough.

So what do we show students? In a season of semester preparation where so much of the work can feel tedious---eternally copy/pasting syllabus policies and endlessly clicking on the same LMS settings---I think it’s worth taking the time to think about our courses. What does a late work policy look like in a class where I am going to tell students to make mistakes and learn from them? How does my assigned homework lead students to make the connections between topics and problems? When in my class sessions do students build and exist in community with each other? Are students encouraged to communicate precisely, and do I assess that kind of communication?

Likely, the goals that you have for the culture that you want to build in your classroom with your students differ from mine (at least a little). But I hope you’re able to ask yourself, even while you settle into the Fall semester, some of these same questions: how are you building this culture in the way you design your courses?

*September 10, 2026*[^OpenAI1]

I guess I'm glad about how things have gone for me in my math career. I can think of two events that seem a lot like failures, but have worked out pretty well.

<ol>
<li>
<p>I washed out of graduate school.</p>

<p>Ok, not really, I guess. Often, when people say this, they mean that they failed out or were asked to leave or didn't pass their comps or some other. That didn't happen to me. I enrolled in a Masters program with the thought that I might continue on to a PhD. One of my mentors had taken a path like this, and I liked the idea of having options. In my Masters program, I convinced myself, true or not, that I wasn't cut out for math research.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>
I've been working at a community college since 2013, teaching math to students in their first two years of college. Then, in 2023, I applied for a job at a SLAC, and was eventually offered a tenure track position. It seemed like a great fit in a small department. I would get to advise math majors with their career plans and introduce them to math research.
</p>
<p>
I eventually turned down the job. At the time, I was worried about job security and salary. So I turned down the (likely only) chance to formally set up a research program with students, albeit a small one.
</p>
</li>
</ol>

Anyways, today, I think it's worked out pretty well. I didn't think that I was cut out for the math research life then, and it's only looking more bleak now. I had a small existential breakdown the other day, trying to figure out what where our field was heading and what kind of future there is for most mathematicians. Most working mathematicians are more like me than the mathematicians working on the Millennium Problems, whether employed by OpenAI or getting scooped by them.

All things considered, I'm in a stable spot compared to many others who have jobs like mine but larger research expectations.

Thank goodness I washed out of that life.

*October 8, 2026*[^OpenAI2]

This isn't *mathematics*. This isn't what our field does or what we value. The result of doing mathematics is not the mathematical results: it's the understanding and knowledge we build through the process of working on these results. And they're out here thinking that this show of force and Lean certification is not only impressive, but accepted as a part of our field? Surely not!

But I'm looking around and while there are a bunch of working mathematicians that are horrified by the direction this field is going, it seems like there are others who are under the misconception that these GenAI companies just need some gentle correction in order to fit the culture of research mathematics. Fit the culture? No way, they're trying to destroy the culture.

As if I can even say that, a guy who doesn't research mathematics and hasn't done any meaningful work in mathematics in years.

*October 22, 2026*

I had 4 students in office hours today. They were from different classes, and all had some different things that they wanted to work on and talk through. This isn't the first time these 4 students have been to office hours, so they have a pretty nice routine.

{: .caption}
![Tea pot with five mugs on a tray.]({{ '/assets/img/posts/tea.png' | relative_url }})
*The aftermath of these office hours.*

When the first student showed up, I asked if they were staying long enough to drink tea: he said he hadn't planned on it, but thought that it might be nice to try a new tea with me that we hadn't had yet. When the kettle had finished boiling the water, 2 more students showed up. They wanted to know what tea I had just started steeping. While the tea steeped, they asked questions about the derivative rules we were learning about while my other student worked through an improper integral so that we could connect it to the infinite series we had talked about.

The 4th student showed up right as I was pouring the tea. He was excited to grab a hot cup, too.

And then, for about an hour and a half, we talked about math. Mostly. It was mostly stuff from our class. But then we would find some connection between the two classes that the students were in, and all of us would talk about that. And we talked about their math experiences and they asked about mine. They wanted to know what graduate school was like, and they wanted to know how to be better at being stuck on problems, and they wanted to know how they wanted to know what problems I had been stuck on recently.

I feel like I don't really *do* mathematics anymore, but this felt more like doing mathematics than anything else.
